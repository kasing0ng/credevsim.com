A sponsor showed his LPs 27.4%. After tax, they keep 21.3%.

A worked teardown of a mobile home park syndication, run through the CREDevSim engine. The pre-tax pitch and the number investors actually pocket are not the same number, and the gap is bigger, and lands differently, than most decks let on.

Kasing Ng, CPA, former Big 4 auditor

The deal: 90-lot manufactured housing park. $4.5M purchase, 65% loan ($2.925M at 6.75%, 25-yr amort), 5-year hold, exit near $6.4M. Waterfall 90/10 with an 8% preferred return. Anonymized composite, not a real named deal.
After-tax LP IRR 21.3% versus 27.4% pre-tax. A 6.1-point drag the pitch deck never underwrites.
Tax at exit $1.37M $854K of it depreciation recapture, taxed as ordinary income, not the 20% most LPs assume.
Cost-seg impact on IRR +0.3 pt A $1.7M year-one deduction moved the after-tax return by three tenths of a point. Here is why.

1. The number in the deck

Pre-tax, this is a strong deal, and the pitch is honest as far as it goes. LP internal rate of return of 27.4%, a 3.03x equity multiple over five years, debt-service coverage comfortably above breakeven. That is the number in the investor summary. It is also the number that stops at the sale line and never crosses into the LP's tax return.

2. The number LPs actually keep

Carry the same deal through federal and state tax at the investor level and the LP return lands at 21.3%, a 2.38x multiple. That is a 6.1-point drag on IRR between the pitch and the pocket. It is not a rounding error and it is not pessimism. It is the tax the pre-tax model simply does not carry.

3. Where the 6.1 points go

Most of the gap shows up in one place: the exit. Total tax at sale runs about $1.37M. Of that, roughly $854K is depreciation recapture, taxed as ordinary income at rates approaching 46% once federal and state stack, not the 20% long-term capital-gains rate most investors have in their heads. The remaining capital-gain tax on appreciation is about $516K. A pre-tax pro forma shows none of this, because it ends before the tax return begins.

4. The cost-seg twist

Here is where the audit habit earns its keep. A mobile home park is an unusually good cost-segregation candidate, because most of the basis is land improvements (roads, pads, utility lines) and park-owned homes, all property with a tax life of 15 years or less, all eligible for 100% bonus depreciation. This park throws off a $1.7M first-year bonus deduction. Real money.

And on a five-year hold, it moved the after-tax LP IRR by three tenths of one point. Not three points. Zero-point-three.

The reason is the same recapture from section three. Every dollar of accelerated depreciation you take up front comes back as ordinary-income recapture when you sell. On a short hold with a taxable exit, the recapture at the end claws back almost the entire time-value benefit of front-loading the deduction. Cost seg still wins if you hold long, if you 1031 into the next deal, or if the passive losses can shelter other income. It is much weaker than the study fee implies on a quick, taxable flip. Nobody selling a cost-seg study shows you that second half.

Why this matters before the raise

When a limited partner or an investment committee asks the obvious question, what do we actually keep after tax, most sponsors do not have the number. The one who walks in already holding it, with the recapture and the passive-loss treatment mapped, looks like the adult in the room.
