3 Things Almost Every CRE Model Gets Wrong About the After-Tax LP Return

By Kasing Ng, CPA — former Big 4 auditor

I spent years as an auditor at a Big 4 firm, on real-estate funds and REITs. Here's what that job actually is: you work in what already happened. The entity is formed, the LPA is signed, the deal is long since closed — and you trace every number in the financial statements back to a source you can point to, because a figure you can't support doesn't belong in the statements. You learn, fast, to distrust any number whose origin you can't see.

That gives you an unusual vantage point on these deals: you see how they actually turned out. The depreciation that was really taken. The recapture that actually hit when the asset sold. The K-1s that actually went out to the LPs. And then you glance back at the pro forma that raised the money in the first place — the forward underwriting sitting in the deal file — and notice it never modeled any of that. It stopped at a pre-tax IRR.

I'm not a developer, and I never underwrote these deals going in. But audit the back end of enough of them and you start seeing the same three gaps between the pitch and the reality — every one on the tax side, every one changing the number the LP actually keeps.

None of the three are hard to understand. Here they are.

Quick note before we start. This isn't an argument against Excel, and it isn't a bet against AI writing your spreadsheets. Use both. This is about the errors that survive inside those tools — the ones a fresh formula or a confident chatbot won't catch for you, because they're errors of what you chose to model, not of arithmetic. Think of this as the second set of eyes, not a replacement for the first.

1. The model stops at pre-tax

This is the big one, and it's almost universal.

Nearly every development pro forma I came across in those deal files ends at a pre-tax IRR and a pre-tax equity multiple. The deck says "17% IRR." Everyone nods. The problem: your LP doesn't spend pre-tax dollars. They spend what's left after depreciation recapture, after capital gains, and — for most passive investors — after the 3.8% net investment income tax.

The gap between the pre-tax number you present and the after-tax number your investor actually receives is not small, and it is not constant across deals. Two deals with an identical 17% pre-tax IRR can hand the LP meaningfully different after-tax returns depending on how much depreciation was taken, how the gain splits at exit, and the investor's own tax posture.

When you show a pre-tax number and call it "the return," you're not lying — but you're showing the LP a number they will never see in their bank account. The sophisticated ones know it, and quietly discount you for it. The unsophisticated ones find out at exit, which is worse.

There's a fair objection here, and good sponsors raise it: you can't model after-tax at screening, because you don't know your LPs yet. One deal takes tax-exempt pension money; the next takes a taxable high-net-worth investor, or a foreign LP behind a blocker. All true — but it proves too much. You don't need anyone's personal 1040 to see the friction that hits every taxable dollar: recapture, capital gains, the 3.8% surtax. The honest move isn't to predict each investor's return — it's to model one standard taxable LP and hold every deal to that same after-tax baseline. And to keep that baseline honest, set it at the top: a top-federal-bracket investor paying the 3.8% NIIT. That's the conservative stress-test floor — if a deal still clears its hurdle after tax under top-bracket assumptions, it's bulletproof, and any lower-bracket or tax-exempt LP can only come out ahead of it. That's what makes it the apples-to-apples floor.

So the fix isn't complicated: carry the deal all the way to that after-tax baseline. Depreciation shields income during the hold; the tax comes due at exit. Model both. It's more work. It's also the number that matters.

2. They lump the recapture together (§1250 vs §1245)

This one is more technical, and it's where I see even good models quietly get it wrong.

When you sell, the depreciation you took gets "recaptured" — the IRS taxes it back. But it doesn't all get taxed the same way, and this is the part spreadsheets flatten:

  • Straight-line depreciation on the real property — the building and its structural components — recaptures under §1250, taxed at a capped 25% rate (the "unrecaptured §1250 gain" rate).
  • Accelerated or bonus depreciation on personal-property components — the 5- and 7-year property a cost-segregation study carves out of the building (carpeting, fixtures, specialized electrical, equipment) — recaptures under §1245, taxed at ordinary income rates plus the 3.8% NIIT for a passive investor. That's north of 40% all-in, not 25%.

If your model takes aggressive cost-segregation or bonus depreciation up front (great for early-year returns) but then recaptures all of it at the 25% §1250 rate at exit, it is understating the exit tax. The early-year benefit looks real; the exit cost is quietly too low; the after-tax IRR is overstated. It's the kind of error that looks fine until an LP's own accountant runs the numbers and asks a question you can't answer in the room.

The fix: split the recapture. §1250 at 25%, §1245 at ordinary + NIIT, and only recapture what you actually accelerated. The two are not interchangeable, and the deals where it matters most are exactly the ones marketed on their depreciation.

3. The number you can't trace

The first two are modeling errors. This one is an audit reflex — the deepest habit the job leaves you with.

In audit, a number you can't trace is a problem by definition. It doesn't matter how reasonable it looks: if you can't follow it back to a source you can point to, it doesn't get signed off. You spend years asking one question until it becomes involuntary — show me where this comes from.

Most underwriting models can't answer it. The final IRR is the output of forty cross-referenced sheets, one of which has a broken link nobody has opened in three months — a liability with a green cell on top. The person presenting it usually isn't dishonest; they just can't see, and can't show, where the number came from.

It surfaces at the worst possible moment: the investment committee, or an LP's analyst, asks where one cash-flow line comes from — and the sponsor is suddenly clicking through tabs, live, hunting for the formula. The number might even be right. It no longer matters. The room just watched them not know their own model.

The fix is a discipline, not a feature: every number that reaches an investor should trace to a formula and an input you can point to. A number you can't show, you don't really know — you're just hoping.
